\section*{Introduction}

In this chapter, we present an alternative reconstruction pipeline to the FMM and CMM methods discussed in Chapters~\ref{chap:FMM} and \ref{chap:CMM}, respectively. While both of these methods rely on forward modelling, this new algorithm, called \emph{Neural Network Map-Making} (NNMM), aims to solve the inverse map-making problem. Although we previously argued that the inverse operator $H^{-1}$ cannot be computed under realistic conditions, NNMM uses physics-guided neural networks to approximate this inverse mapping. Following this approach, we develop inverse counterparts of both FMM and CMM based on neural networks.

We first compare the advantages and limitations of inverse methods with those of the forward-modelling approaches presented in the previous chapters. We then introduce the key concepts underlying the inversion procedure. Finally, we address one of the main limitations of neural networks in physics applications, namely uncertainty estimation, by combining physics-guided neural networks with the probabilistic programming framework Pyro\footnote{\url{https://pyro.ai/}}.

This chapter is intentionally kept concise, as the draft of the associated publication is included immediately afterwards.

\section{Inverse problem approach}

In the FMM chapter, we claimed in the subsection~\ref{sub:mm_solution} that it was not numerically possible to solve the equation (see equation~\ref{eq:mapmaking_inv} for details):

\begin{equation}
    \vec{\hat{s}} = (H^{~T} N^{-1} H)^{-1} H^{~T} N^{-1} \vec{d},
\end{equation}
because it is too expensive to invert the instrumental model $H$ explicitely. To overcome this difficulty, Leonora Kardum had the idea of separating each sub-operator that composed $H$, detailed in the section \ref{section:instrumental_model}, to compute their individual inverse either through analytical expression when possible or pseudo-inverse estimation. Otherwise, Machine Learning is used to approximate inversion for too complicated operators.

Therefore, the goal of this approach is to compute the inverse expression of all the operators describing the instrument systems, to build $H^{-1}$. From equation \ref{eq:instrumental_model}, we have:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{H}_{\nu}^{-1} = \mathcal{U}_{\text{nit}, \nu}^{-1} \mathcal{T}_{\text{atm}}^{-1} \mathcal{A}_{\text{perture}}^{-1} \mathcal{F}_{\text{ilt}}^{-1} [\mathcal{P}_{\text{roj}, \nu}^{-1} * \mathcal{H}_{\text{WP}}^{-1}] \mathcal{P}_{\text{ol}}^{-1} \mathcal{I}_{\text{nt}}^{-1} \mathcal{T}_{\text{rans}}^{-1} \mathcal{R}_{\text{esp}}^{-1}.
    \label{eq:inv_instrumental_model}
\end{equation}

The inverse approach has multiple benefits. The first and main one is regarding its versatility. While the forward approach depends on the TOD, and needs to be re-done for each data set, the inverse can be computed once and applied for each data set. Additionally, because of the modular operators' construction, if one element from equation \ref{eq:inv_instrumental_model} happens to be wrongly estimated, only this one needs to be computed again, and not the entire instrumental model. For the same reason, operators can be added or removed from $H$ easily (in case of modification of the instrumental model). 

\section{Operators inversion}

The operators composing the instrumental model are not all inverted in the same manner. Depending on their mathematical properties, three different strategies are adopted. For a more detailed description, please refer to the draft article link with this chapter.

\textbf{Analytical inversion.}
Several operators correspond to simple diagonal or scalar transformations and can therefore be inverted analytically. This is the case for the unit conversion $\mathcal{U}_{\text{nit}}$, atmospheric transmission $\mathcal{T}_{\text{atm}}$, aperture integration $\mathcal{A}_{\text{perture}}$, filtering $\mathcal{F}_{\text{ilt}}$, detector integration $\mathcal{I}_{\text{nt}}$, and transmission $\mathcal{T}_{\text{rans}}$ operators. Their inverse simply consists of an element-wise scaling and is implemented as either a fixed or a learnable diagonal layer.

\textbf{Model-based inversion.}
Some operators are not diagonal but are described by well-established physical models that provide an analytical inverse under appropriate assumptions. This applies to the bolometer time-response operator $\mathcal{R}_{\text{esp}}$ and to the polarisation rotation operators $\mathcal{P}_{\text{ol}}$ and $\mathcal{H}_{\text{WP}}$. Rather than learning these transformations from scratch, the network embeds their analytical inverse while treating the associated instrumental quantities, such as the detector time constant $\tau$, as differentiable physical parameters.

\textbf{Learned correction of a pseudo-inverse.}
The projection operator $\mathcal{P}_{\text{roj}, \nu}$ is the only operator for which no analytical inverse can be defined. Since it maps the sky onto the time-ordered data with incomplete and non-uniform sampling, the inverse problem is intrinsically ill-posed. As a first approximation, we compute the standard maximum-likelihood back-projection,
\[
\mathcal{P}_{\text{roj}}^\dagger = (\mathcal{P}_{\text{roj}}^T N^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{\text{roj}})^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{\text{roj}}^TN^{-1},
\]
which is itself approximated by its diagonal form $\mathcal{P}_{\text{roj}}^T1$. Instead of requiring the neural network to reconstruct the full inverse operator, it is only trained to estimate the correction associated with the neglected off-diagonal couplings.

\section{Uncertainty estimation}

From the three methods to invert the operators, one can see that we are relying on the physical description of each operator to compute their inverse (except for the projection operator). Our goal is not to use machine learning as a black box to compute $H^{-1}$, we want to keep the interpretability as well as the uncertainty estimation of key instrumental parameters by applying this method.

Each analytically or model-based inverted operator depends on one or several physical quantities, such as the detector efficiency $\eta$, the beam solid angle $\Omega$, or the effective horn radius $r$, that are only known from calibration or optical simulations, and therefore carry a systematic uncertainty. Rather than fixing these quantities to their nominal calibrated value, we treat them as latent random variables and infer their posterior distribution directly from the TOD, using the probabilistic programming framework Pyro~\cite{bingham2019pyro}, built on top of PyTorch\footnote{\url{https://pytorch.org/}}.

\textbf{Probabilistic model.}
For a given inverse operator layer $\mathcal{L}_\theta$ acting on a parameter $\theta$ (e.g.\ $\theta = \eta$ for the transmission layer), we enforce positivity by working in log-space and place a Gaussian prior on $\log\theta$,
\begin{equation}
    \log\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2),
\end{equation}
where $\mu_0$ and $\sigma_0$ are set from the calibrated value and its expected uncertainty. Given the detector TOD $\vec{d}$ before the operator under study has been inverted, the layer produces a corrected estimate $\hat{\vec{s}} = \mathcal{L}_\theta(\vec{d})$, which is compared to the TOD $\vec{s}$ obtained after inversion in the noiseless simulation chain through a Gaussian likelihood,
\begin{equation}
    s \mid \theta \sim \mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{L}_\theta(\vec{d}),\, \sigma_n^2\big),
    \label{eq:pyro_likelihood}
\end{equation}
with $\sigma_n$ a fixed observation noise controlling how tightly $\hat{\vec{s}}$ is required to match $\vec{s}$. This mirrors, operator by operator, the forward simulation pipeline of Section~\ref{section:instrumental_model}, providing paired $(d, \vec{s})$ examples on which the parameter can be calibrated.

\textbf{Variational inference.}
The posterior $p(\theta \mid d, s)$ has no closed form once $\mathcal{L}_\theta$ is non-linear in $\theta$, so it is approximated with Stochastic Variational Inference (SVI). A mean-field Gaussian guide $q_\phi(\log\theta) = \mathcal{N}(m_\phi, s_\phi^2)$ is fit to the true posterior by maximising the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) with respect to the variational parameters $\phi = (m_\phi, s_\phi)$, using the Adam optimiser. In practice this only requires specifying the generative model of Eq.~\ref{eq:pyro_likelihood} as a Pyro module; the guide and the gradient-based optimisation of the ELBO are handled automatically by Pyro's \texttt{AutoNormal} guide and \texttt{SVI} routines.

\textbf{Posterior estimation.}
Once trained, the variational distribution $q_\phi(\log\theta)$ is used as a proxy for the true posterior. Drawing samples from it and exponentiating gives samples of $\theta$ itself, from which we estimate a posterior mean and standard deviation per detector. The spread of this posterior directly quantifies how well the corresponding instrumental parameter, and hence the associated inverse operator, is constrained by the data, and can be propagated as a systematic uncertainty on the reconstructed sky signal. This procedure is applied independently to each parametrised operator (transmission, detector integration, aperture integration, ...), and is designed to generalise to any operator of the inverse chain by simply supplying its forward expression and prior.
