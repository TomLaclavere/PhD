\section*{Introduction}

While astrophysical foreground B-mode emission is a fundamental limitation for the detection of primordial B-modes in the CMB signal, ground-based experiments are also contaminated by atmospheric emission. Atmosphere represents the main contributions of the photon noise, due to its high temperature comparing to the sky emissions. Additionally, this noise exhibits temporal and spatial correlations, creating contaminations for the map-making process. Mitigating its effects is one of the most challenging, yet essential, steps toward the detection of primordial B-modes with next-generation CMB experiments.

This chapter presents a new method, based on the interferometric properties of the QUBIC instrument, to remove atmospheric contamination from CMB data using an extended version of the Component Map-Making algorithm introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:CMM}. It is organised into six sections. We begin with an introduction to the key properties of the atmosphere, the model used to describe it, and the software developed to simulate it. Next, we discuss the properties of atmospheric emission that are particularly problematic for CMB observations, namely its temporal and spatial correlations. We then describe the modifications required to the data acquisition model, followed by the presentation of the different application cases studied. The results are then analysed, allowing us to identify the limitations of using CMM to separate atmospheric and CMB signals. Finally, an alternative model is introduced to address some of the limitations of the previous approach. \\

\subsection{Atmosphere properties}

At millimeter wavelenghts, the atmosphere is the main contaminant for astrophysical and cosmological observations. Observing through the atmosphere is the source of many problems, associated with its properties. They are characterised by the temperature, the presssure, and more importantly, the Precipitable Water Vapour (PWV) of the atmosphere. The PWV is defined from the water vapour density at the considered location through:

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{PWV} = \frac{1}{\rho_w}\int_0^{\infty}\rho_v(z)\,dz,
\end{equation}
where $\rho_w$ is the density of liquid water ($1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$), and $\rho_v(z)$ is the water vapour density at altitude $z$.

The first difficulty is that atmosphere is not a transparant medium as the frequency range. Its transmission spectrum, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:atm_transmitivity}, contains absorption lines from $O_2$ and $H_2 O$, limiting the observation of the sky between these lines. For these purpose, QUBIC instrument is observing between $130$ and $170$ GHz, as well as between $190$ and $245$ GHz. Additionally, the transmittance is increasing at high frequency, even within the observing windows with the PWV. This is the main motivation for observing from a dry site at high altitude, which has the effect of decreasing the water vapour density $\rho$.
