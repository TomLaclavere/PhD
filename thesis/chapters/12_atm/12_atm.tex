\section*{Introduction}

While astrophysical foreground B-mode emission is a fundamental limitation for the detection of primordial B-modes in the CMB signal, ground-based experiments are also contaminated by atmospheric emission. Atmosphere represents the main contributions of the photon noise, due to its high temperature comparing to the sky emissions. Additionally, this noise exhibits temporal and spatial correlations, creating contaminations for the map-making process. Mitigating its effects is one of the most challenging, yet essential, steps toward the detection of primordial B-modes with next-generation CMB experiments.

This chapter presents a new method, based on the interferometric properties of the QUBIC instrument, to remove atmospheric contamination from CMB data using an extended version of the Component Map-Making algorithm introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:CMM}. It is organised into six sections. We begin with an introduction to the key properties of the atmosphere, the model used to describe it, and the software developed to simulate it. Next, we discuss the properties of atmospheric emission that are particularly problematic for CMB observations, namely its temporal and spatial correlations. We then describe the modifications required to the data acquisition model, followed by the presentation of the different application cases studied. The results are then analysed, allowing us to identify the limitations of using CMM to separate atmospheric and CMB signals. Finally, an alternative model is introduced to address some of the limitations of the previous approach. \\

\section{Atmosphere model \& Simulation} \label{section:atm_simulation}

We introduced in Subsection~\ref{subsection:atm} a preliminary description of atmospheric properties and the associated challenges for CMB observations. In this section, we provide a more detailed description, as well as the simulation software developed during this thesis.

\subsection{Atmosphere properties}

At millimetre wavelengths, the atmosphere is the main source of contamination for astrophysical and cosmological observations. Observations through the atmosphere are affected by several physical properties, affected by the atmosphere temperature, pressure, and more importantly the precipitable water vapour (PWV). The PWV is defined from the water vapour density at a given location as:

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{PWV} = \frac{1}{\rho_w} \int_0^{\infty} \rho_v(z)\, dz,
\end{equation}
where $\rho_w$ is the density of liquid water ($1000\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$), and $\rho_v(z)$ is the water vapour density at altitude $z$.

\subsubsection{Transmitivity}

The first difficulty is that the atmosphere is not a fully transparent medium across the relevant frequency range. Its transmission spectrum, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:atm_transmitivity}, contains absorption lines from $O_2$ and $H_2O$, limiting observations to the atmospheric windows between these lines. For this reason, the QUBIC instrument operates in two bands, between $131$ and $169$ GHz and between $192.5$ and $247.5$ GHz. Additionally, the atmospheric transmittance decreases at higher frequencies even within these windows due to PWV absorption. This is one of the main motivations for observing from a dry, high-altitude site, which reduces the water vapour density $\rho_v$.

\subsubsection{Brightness}

In addition, atmospheric brightness, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:atm_brightness_bis}, is a major source of noise. The higher the brightness temperature, the larger the photon noise induced in the detectors. It also contributes to the thermal load of the detectors, meaning that it is more challenging to cool down the detectors at the temperature required for superconductivity while observing through atmosphere. The brightness also scales with the PWV at the observation site, further reinforcing the need for a dry location.

\subsubsection{Fluctuations}

Despite its mean emission, the atmosphere is a dynamic and turbulent medium with fluctuations in its water vapour density distribution. These fluctuations lead to temporal and spatial correlations in the observed time-ordered data (TOD) that will bias the maps' reconstruction. 
The water vapour density fluctuations are assumed to follow the Kolmogorov model~\cite{Kolmogorov1941,Tatarski1961}, characterised by the tridmensional power-spectrum:

\begin{equation}
    P(\vec{k}) \propto |\vec{k}|^{-11/3}.
\end{equation}

However, this spectrum is not integrable at low spatial frequencies, which implies that the fully scale-invariant turbulence cannot extend to arbitrarily large spactial scales. In practice, this is regularised by introducing a low-wavenumber cutoff $k_\text{min} = r_0^{-1}$, where $r_0^{-1}$ represents the largest scale over which the turbulence can be considered scale-invariant~\cite{morris2022}. This leads to the modified spectrum:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:Kolmogorov}
    P_\text{adj}(\vec{k}) \propto (r_0^{-2} + |\vec{k}|^2)^{-11/6},
\end{equation}
which interpolates between a constant behaviour at low $k$ and the Kolmogorov turbulences at high $k$. The proportional constant is chosen so that the spectrum is normalised, as:

\begin{equation}
    \int P_\text{adj}(\vec{k})d\vec{k}=1.
\end{equation}

\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Polarisation}

The emission from the atmosphere is considered to not be polarised. However, few effects are producing polarised emission, such that the Zeeman splitting of $O_2$ by the Hearth magnetic field~\cite{hanany2003, spinelli2011, petroff2020, fujino2025} or the ice cristals in clouds~\cite{takakura2019, li2023, coerver2025}.

The Zeeman effect occurs as the Earth's magnetic filed splits the energy levels of $O_2$ molecules, so their emission comes out slightly circularly polarised. The signal is tied to the magnetic field direction. The effect is relatively small: typical circular polarisation amplitudes are $\sim$130–500 $\mu$K depending on site and frequency \cite{petroff2020,fabbian2012}, corresponding to a fraction of order $10^{-4}$--$10^{-5}$ of the total intensity emission. It also produces a tiny fraction of linear polarisation, about $10000$ times smaller than the circular polarisation ($\sim 1$--$10$~nK)~\cite{fabbian2012}. This effect is deterministic and tied to a fixed geomagnetic pattern, and can therefore be modeled and subtracted as a template, rather than requiring dedicated hardware.

Some clouds, called $\emph{cirrus clouds}$, are thin clouds present at high altitude, transparent to CMB experiment at millimetre range. Nevertheless, they yield horizontally-aligned ice cristals which backward scatter ground radiation and forward scatter sky signal, producing a linear polarisation emission~\cite{li2023}. Unlike Zeeman signal, this signal acts as a \enquote{burst} when a cirrus clouds is observed by the instrument, meaning that it can't be averaged. These events need to be flaged, using sky camera or LIDAR for example, and treated in post analysis. When observing through ice clouds, the atmosphere polarisation emission can reach about $5$--$10\%$ of the intensity emision ($\lesssim 0.1$ K)~\cite{takakura2019}.

During this study, we are completely negleting these effects, and consider the atmosphere to be unpolarised. There is no fundamental limitation for including atmosphere polarisation, but as explained, the expected polarisation is too low to be properly reconstructed with our methods, and is therefore dismissed. It can be an important extension of this work to add atmosphere polarisation effect.

\subsection{Numerical simulation}

Now that we have described the various effects of the atmosphere that are problematic for CMB observations, we present the simulation software used to generate atmospheric emission. Since our objective is to apply the CMM algorithm to atmospheric contamination, we first need to simulate maps of the atmosphere.
The state-of-the-art software for modelling the mean atmospheric properties is the \emph{am} model~\cite{paine2024}. However, \emph{am} is limited to modelling the average atmospheric properties along a given line of sight and does not include turbulent fluctuations. Additional models are therefore required to simulate atmospheric turbulence. Two main tools, both based on the formalism introduced in the previously discussed studies, have been developed for this purpose.

The most widely adopted is \emph{TOAST}\footnote{\url{https://toast-cmb.readthedocs.io/en/latest/intro.html}} (Time Ordered Astrophysics Scalable Tools)~\cite{TOAST}, a framework for simulating and processing time-ordered data for experiments such as Planck, LiteBIRD, Simons Array, and the Simons Observatory. Its atmospheric module generates a full three-dimensional turbulent volume following the formalism of~\cite{errard2015}. This volume is then advected according to the wind velocity and propagated through the focal plane, reproducing both the temporal correlations along the scan and the spatial correlations between detectors. The implementation is designed to scale to the hundreds of thousands of detectors expected in next-generation CMB experiments.
A more recent and lightweight alternative is \emph{maria}\footnote{\url{https://thomaswmorris.com/maria/index.html}}~\cite{maria}, a general-purpose (sub-)millimetre observation simulator developed for survey forecasting across a range of single-dish facilities, including MUSTANG-2, AtLAST, and several configurations of ACT, SPT, and the Simons Observatory. Rather than simulating a full three-dimensional turbulent volume, \emph{maria} relies on the two-dimensional frozen-turbulence approximation introduced by~\cite{morris2022}, in which the atmosphere is represented as a set of thin, wind-advected turbulent layers. This approximation enables significantly more efficient simulations of long scanning observations, at the expense of a simplified turbulence model. In addition, \emph{maria} couples this turbulence model with site- and time-dependent weather conditions derived from the ECMWF ERA5 reanalysis database and has been successfully validated against observations from MUSTANG-2~\cite{maria}.
Both tools rely on the same calibration of the turbulence outer scale derived from POLARBEAR and ACT observations~\cite{errard2015}. They also share conceptual similarities with the simulation approach adopted in this thesis, which favours a simplified, self-contained implementation of the Kolmogorov turbulence model rather than the full TOAST framework.

Unfortunately, these frameworks were developed for classical imagers and cannot be directly applied to the specific case of QUBIC\footnote{In her PhD thesis, Louise Mousset proposed an approach to adapt TOAST to the QUBIC instrument. Such an implementation would greatly facilitate the development of realistic simulations, as TOAST already provides many advanced features, particularly for modelling instrumental systematics. However, this approach was not explored further due to time constraints~\cite{thesislouise}.}. Instead, we chose to develop our own simplified atmospheric model, inspired by the formalisms of~\cite{errard2015} and~\cite{morris2022}, rather than investing significant effort into adapting TOAST or \emph{maria} to QUBIC. The assumptions and implemented features of this model are described in the following.

To account for the impact of atmospheric emission on QUBIC's observations, we simulate the atmosphere as a thin, turbulent layer of water vapor above the instrument\footnote{The software also allows to simulate a 3D volume, similarly than TOAST, but which cannot be used for data acquisition because $H$ only accept HEALPix maps.}. The simulation proceeds in four stages: computing the mean physical properties of the atmosphere as a function of altitude, generating spatially-correlated fluctuations of the water vapor density that mimic atmospheric turbulence, converting these fluctuations into a frequency-dependent brightness temperature using the atmosphere's absorption spectrum, and simulating the temporal evolution of the atmosphere through wind advection. The simulation code is added in the \emph{qubicsoft} library\footnote{\url{https://github.com/qubicsoft/qubic/blob/master/qubic/lib/MapMaking/Qatmosphere.py}}, but is not implemented in the simulation and reconstruction pipeline yet.

\subsubsection{Physical properties}

The main physical properties responsible for the atmospheric emission are the temperature and the water vapour density. They are described by two mean quantities that decreased exponentially with altitude $h$ :

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Temperature: } $T_{atm}(h) = T_{atm}(0) e^{-h/h_T}$, where $T_{atm}(0)$ is the average ground temperature and $h_T$ a characteristic scale height~\cite{morris2022}.
    \item \textbf{Water vapour density: } $\langle \rho(h) \rangle = \rho_0 e^{-\ln 2 \frac{(h-5190)}{h_{H_2O}}}$, where $\rho_0$ is a reference density at 5190 m, the QUBIC altitude, and $h_{H_2O}$ the water vapour half-height.
\end{itemize}

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{atm_temp_rho.png}
    \caption{(left) Mean atmosphere temperature and (right) mean water vapour density with altitude. QUBIC altitude is illustrated with the black dashed line. Parameters value are taken from \cite{morris2022}.}
    \label{fig:atm_temp_rho}
\end{figure}

As no advanced measurement of PWV has been performed at QUBIC observing site, we are using results from Atacama Desert, located at $\sim 300$ km from QUBIC and widely documented, to initialise the simulation parameters. This can be adjusted future with on-site measurements.

\subsubsection{Fluctuations}

After defining the average properties of the atmosphere, we need to implement its fluctuation, following the Kolmogorov power spectrum, from equation~\ref{eq:Kolmogorov}. During this procedure, we faced an important limitation: the Kolmogorov spectrum is written for Cartesian unit, while our acquisition operator $H$ needs HEALPix map. We developed two alternative implementations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Flat-sky (Fourier) method: }random phases are drawn on a 2D Fourier grid, weighted by $\sqrt{P(\mathbf{k})}$, and inverse-Fourier-transformed to real space to obtain a fluctuation map $\delta\rho(x,y)$. This flat map is then projected into a healpix map, which create geometrical distortions far from the zenith. 
    \item \textbf{Full-sky (spherical harmonics) method: }the Kolmogorov spectrum is first translated into an angular correlation function $C(\theta)$ (using the relation between real-space separation and pointing angle, $r = 2h_{atm}\sin(\theta/2)$), then converted into an angular power spectrum $C_\ell$ (computed with CAMB), from which a HEALPix map is drawn with synfast function from healpy. This method is directly creating a HEALPix map of the atmosphere without projection, but it is not physically motivated to represent the atmophere as the surface of a sphere centered on the observer. This representation still may be correct close to the zenith, but may be treated carefully regarding the comparison with the 2D case.
\end{itemize}

Both approaches produce a fluctuation map $\delta\rho$ representing the turbulent structure of the water vapor field at a given time.

\textbf{Remark: }Both methods to create an atmospheric map compatible with the QUBIC acquisition operator present potential problems. It is a current limitation from our current simulation software, totally out of the scope of this thesis, which will requiere extra attention in the future.

Most atmospheric publications do not measure directly the fluctuations of the water vapour density, but mainly the fluctuations of PWV. We are using this standard deviation $\sigma_{PWV}$ to compute a density fluctuation scale $\sigma_\rho = \sigma_\text{PWV}/h$ (with $h = 2 h_{H_2O}$), following a simple scale approximation~\cite{sugiyama2024}.

\subsubsection{Frequency dependancy}    

The absorbing power of the atmosphere is captured by an absorption spectrum. It is built from precomputed transmission tables (generated with the \emph{am} atmospheric model~\cite{paine2024}) combining three contributions: line-by-line molecular absorption from $H_2O$ and $O_2$, and two continuum terms from $H_2O$–$H_2O$ self-collisions and $H_2O$–air collisions. Combined with the local gas densities (computed via CoolProp\footnote{\url{https://coolprop.org/}} from pressure and temperature), these give the absorption coefficient $\alpha_b(\nu) = \frac{1}{m_{H_2O}}(k_\text{lines} + n_{H_2O}k_\text{self}(\nu) + n_\text{air}k_\text{air}(\nu))$, in $m^2$/g, which quantifies how efficiently the atmosphere absorbs/emits radiation at frequency $\nu$. The result is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:atm_spectrum}. An example of the integrated subbands is also displayed for $N_\text{sub}=8$. In practice, this spectrum is also used to compute the atmosphere mixing matrix elements for the CMM algorithm.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{atm_spectrum.png}
    \caption{Atmosphere absorption spectrum computed using \emph{am}. The QUBIC frequency bands at 150 and 220 GHz are shown, as well as the subbands integration with their relative bandwidth.}
    \label{fig:atm_spectrum}
\end{figure}

The spectrum is adjusted, depending on PWV, temperature and pressure. 

\subsubsection{Atmosphere emission}

From all these elements, we can compute the atmosphere emission, in Kelvin, from: 

\begin{equation}
    dT(\vec{r}, \nu) = \alpha_b(\nu) \rho(\vec{r}) B(\vec{r}, \nu) T_\text{atm}(\vec{r})dV, 
\end{equation}
with $\vec{r}$ the pointing direction, $\alpha_b$ the absorption spectrum, $\rho$ the water vapour density, $B$ the instrument beam, and $T_\text{atm}$ the temperature of the atmosphere. By integrating this expression on the volumen seen by the beam, we have the emission of the atmosphere in Kelvin. 

As bolometers are sensible to temperature fluctuations, we can replace $\rho$ by the fluctuation map $\delta\rho$, which builds the temperature map of the atmosphere $\delta T$, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:atm_map}.

\subsubsection{Wind}

The final atmospheric property to be modeled is the advection induced by the wind. Two methods have been developed to account for this effect.

The most intuitive approach consists of directly advecting the simulated atmospheric map. The entire realisation is shifted by an amount corresponding to the physical displacement induced by the wind, and the telescope samples this shifted map instead of the original one. In practice, every point of the sky map is displaced by the same angular offset, obtained by converting the physical wind displacement (accumulated over time, in metres) into an angular quantity using the altitude of the atmospheric layer as the relevant length scale. Each point is moved along the shortest path on the sphere (a great circle) by an angle equal to the magnitude of this shift. A complete mathematical description of this method, known as the \emph{exponential map}, can be found in \cite{boumal2023} (Example~5.37 and Section~10.21). The displacement is applied in the direction opposite to the wind since, for each point of the shifted map, we seek the location on the original map from which its content originated. The shifted map is then obtained by interpolating the original realisation at these displaced positions, independently for each frequency and Stokes parameter. Although conceptually straightforward, this approach is computationally expensive because it must be applied to every pixel of the map.

To reduce this computational cost, we developed a simpler alternative in which the pointing directions are shifted instead of the atmospheric map itself, which therefore remains unchanged. This approach relies on the frozen-screen hypothesis, commonly used to describe atmospheric turbulence, according to which a time-evolving, wind-advected atmosphere is equivalent to a single frozen realisation observed from displaced positions. Rather than recomputing the entire atmospheric map at every time sample, only the much smaller set of pointing directions sampled by the instrument is shifted, making this the default and considerably more efficient implementation.

The wind is modeled as a cumulative horizontal displacement that increases throughout the observation. For a constant wind velocity, the displacement grows linearly with time, whereas for a fluctuating wind it follows a random walk. Two geometrical implementations are used, depending on the atmospheric representation. When the atmosphere is modeled as a flat layer, the wind-induced displacement is computed exactly: each pointing direction is projected onto the atmospheric plane, the horizontal wind displacement is added directly to the projected position, and the resulting coordinates are projected back onto the celestial sphere. When the atmosphere is represented by a full-sky map, the same physical displacement is converted into a single angular shift, assumed to be uniform across the sky and independent of the pointing elevation. This angular displacement is then applied along the surface of the sphere, following the shortest path and in the direction opposite to the wind, consistently with the map-advection method described above.

Both implementations are mathematically equivalent under these assumptions, as demonstrated in Figure~\ref{fig:atm_wind}. This equivalence has been verified using high-resolution maps and for sky regions sufficiently far from the poles, where projection distortions remain negligible. At coarser resolution, the difference between the methods is more important, due to pixelisation effect, but not related to the code. More robust implementations could be developed in the future, for example by relying on the \texttt{Rotator}\footnote{\url{https://healpy.readthedocs.io/en/latest/generated/healpy.rotator.Rotator.html}} class provided by \emph{healpy}, or the function \emph{shift} from SciPy \texttt{ndimage}\footnote{\url{https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.ndimage.shift.html}}.

\textbf{Remark.} The inclusion of wind during data simulation and map reconstruction is discussed later in this chapter. Wind acts sequentially, continuously advecting the atmosphere between successive observations, whereas the acquisition operator $H$ computes the full time-ordered data for all pointing directions simultaneously. Consequently, particular care is required when incorporating wind into the acquisition model.
