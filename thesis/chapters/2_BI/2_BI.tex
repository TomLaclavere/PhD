\chapter{QUBIC: A Bolometric Interferometer (BI)}
\minitoc

\section*{Introduction}

Observations of the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) have become one of the most powerful probes in modern cosmology, providing stringent constraints on the composition, geometry, and evolution of the Universe. In particular, the polarisation of the CMB carries unique information about the physics of the early Universe. Of special interest is the curl-like component of the polarisation field, known as $B$-modes, which can be generated by primordial tensor perturbations produced during Inflation. The detection of such primordial $B$-modes would provide compelling evidence for the quantum origin of cosmological fluctuations and offer a direct probe of the energy scale of Inflation.
However, measuring $B$-mode polarisation remains extremely challenging. The expected signal is several orders of magnitude weaker than the temperature anisotropies and is significantly contaminated by astrophysical foregrounds, such as Galactic dust and synchrotron emission, as well as by instrumental systematics. Achieving the required sensitivity and control over these systematics therefore necessitates innovative experimental strategies.

The Q\&U Bolometric Interferometer for Cosmology (QUBIC\footnote{\url{https://www.qubic.org.ar/}})~\cite{QUBICI} has been designed to address these challenges by combining the high sensitivity of bolometric detectors with the intrinsic systematic control of interferometric techniques. Unlike traditional imaging experiments, QUBIC directly measures the interference pattern produced by an array of horns. This approach provides multiple independent constraints on the instrument response, which can be exploited through a self-calibration procedure to mitigate instrumental systematics, discussed further in Chapter \ref{chap:gps}. \jc{In addition, the interferometric nature of the instrument naturally produces a frequency-dependent synthesized beam, which encodes spectral information directly in the measured data. This property enables the spectral imaging capabilities of QUBIC and allows a significant improvement in map-making and component-separation performance, as discussed in Chapter~\ref{chap:spectral_imaging}.}

In this chapter, we present the QUBIC experiment in detail. We first describe the instrumental concept and its key components, with particular emphasis on the principles of bolometric interferometry. We then discuss the scientific objectives of QUBIC. Finally, we present the main instrumental subsystems of the telescope, along with their respective roles and limitations.

The QUBIC collaboration published a series of eight papers in 2022 describing the instrument in detail. These articles cover the overall project~\cite{QUBICI}, its unique spectral capabilities~\cite{QUBICII}, and comprehensive descriptions of its key instrumental components\sout{~\cite{QUBICIII, QUBICIV, QUBICV, QUBICVI, QUBICVII, QUBICVIII}} : \tom{instrument laboratory characterisation~\cite{QUBICIII}, performance of detectors and electronic readout~\cite{QUBICIV}, cryogenic system~\cite{QUBICV}, Half Wave Plate rotator system~\cite{VI}, back-to-back horns array~\cite{QUBICVII} and the optical design and specific beam~\cite{QUBICVIII}}. They form the foundation of this chapter.

\section{Telescopes for CMB physics}

\subsection{Classical approach: Imager}

Before introducing QUBIC instrument, it is interesting to first present the design adopted by most modern CMB experiments, called \emph{direct imaging}. Imagers are long-exposure telescopes directly forming images of the sky on their focal planes, where detectors are located. Imagers directly measure the sky intensity projected onto the focal plane, producing sky maps after scanning and reconstruction. This design is widely adopted by modern CMB experiments such as Planck~\cite{PlanckDesign}, Bicep2/Keck Array~\cite{BicepDesign}, Simons Observatory~\cite{SODesign} or LiteBird~\cite{LiteBirdDesign}. 

The angular resolution is fundamentally limited by diffraction, and scales as :
\begin{equation}
    \theta \sim \frac{\lambda}{D},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is the observing wavelength and $D$ the telescope diameter. As a consequence, large aperture telescopes are required to observe small angular scales.

These experiments use thousands of detectors, each observing a small patch of the sky independently. These detectors measure the total incoming power from photons. CMB polarisation observations require \jc{placing detectors after a polariser, or using specific polarisation-sensitive detectors, that need to be } extremely sensitive detectors, operating close to the photon noise limit. \tom{The photon noise is a fundamental statistical noise, intrinsically limiting detectors' sensitivity. It consists into two terms : \emph{shot noise} coming from the Poisson fluctuations of the discrete arrival of photons, scaling proportionally to frequency and photon flux, and \emph{wave noise} due to their boson nature, photons arrive at the detector in correlated bunches.} The dominant technology used in modern CMB instruments is the bolometer, currently equipping nearly all major experiments\footnote{Another type of detector called Kinetic Inductance Detectors (KIDs) is currently at the centre of many R\&D projects, in order to replace bolometers in future CMB experiments~\cite{KIDs, KIDs_Overview}. KIDs are already used in instruments such as NIKA2~\cite{NIKA2} and TolTEC~\cite{tolTEC}.}. Bolometers measure the variation of resistance caused by an increase of temperature of a thermal mass after absorption of incoming radiation (see Figure~\ref{fig:bolometer}).

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.28\linewidth]{Bolometer.pdf}
    \caption{Diagram of a bolometer. Incoming radiation is absorbed by the absorber, in thermal equilibrium with the thermal mass, leading to an increase of its temperature. This modifies the resistivity of the material, which can be measured in order to estimate the total incoming power.}
    \label{fig:bolometer}
\end{figure}

As detectors observe different regions of the sky independently, the ideal white-noise sensitivity improves with the number of detectors. Assuming uncorrelated white noise, the noise RMS of one detector scales as :
\begin{equation}
    \sigma \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{t}},
\end{equation}
where $t$ is the observing time. For $N_{\mathrm{det}}$ independent detectors observing simultaneously, the sensitivity therefore scales as :
\begin{equation}
    \sigma \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{det}} t_{\mathrm{obs}}}}.
\end{equation}

As a consequence, modern CMB experiments aim at maximizing the number of detectors populating the focal plane, representing a large fraction of the instrument complexity and cost. The number of detectors has dramatically increased over the last decade\footnote{Bicep2/Keck Array (2015): $\sim 3000$ detectors, Simons Observatory (2025): $\sim 60\,000$ detectors.}.

\subsection{Innovative design: Bolometric Interferometry}

Interferometry was the dominant design for several CMB experiments in the early 2000s. Important examples include VSA~\cite{VSA}, DASI~\cite{DASI} or CBI~\cite{CBI}. Interferometers can be separated into two main categories: \emph{Coherent Interferometry} (CI), \jc{also called \emph{Heterodyne Interferometry}}, and \emph{Bolometric Interferometry} (BI).

In coherent interferometers, the instrument measures correlations between antennas, similarly to classical radio interferometry. Each antenna receives an electric field $E_i(t)$, which is amplified and pairwise correlated with the signals from other antennas through complex correlators. The measured quantity is called the \emph{visibility} :
\begin{equation}
    V(u,v) = \langle E_i E_j^\ast \rangle,
\end{equation}
which corresponds to a Fourier mode of the sky. One major advantage of this approach is its excellent control of systematic effects, due to the direct comparison between baselines. Interferometers also direct access to Fourier modes of the sky, directly related to the power spectrum. In addition, their optical design is generally simpler than large imaging telescopes.

However, coherent interferometers suffer from several major limitations. First, amplifier noise complicates high-sensitivity measurements at CMB frequencies. \jc{The correlators also suffer from a very limited bandwidth: many are needed to observe enough photons}. More importantly, the number of correlators scales as :
\begin{equation}
    N_{\mathrm{corr}} \sim N_{\mathrm{ant}}^2,
\end{equation}
making large interferometric arrays extremely expensive and technically complex. This scalability problem is the main reason why direct imagers progressively replaced coherent interferometers in modern CMB experiments. \jc{As an example, one correlator costs $\sim 5 k\$ $ meaning that for $100$ antennas, it will cost $\sim 25 M\$ $ to equip the $5000$ channels. For $1000$ antennas, the cost raises to $2.5 G\$ $.}
\rmk{Maybe footnote ?}

Bolometric interferometry aims at combining the advantages of interferometry with the high sensitivity of bolometric detectors. In BIs, electronic correlators are replaced by an optical combiner, naturally producing interference patterns from many baselines simultaneously. Amplifiers are also removed, and the signal is directly measured using high-sensitivity bolometers. In bolometric interferometers, the different baselines are produced using an array of horns, which can be seen as an array of Young's holes~\cite{young1804}. The signals are then combined through an optical system composed of large mirrors, focusing the resulting interference pattern onto a focal plane where detectors are located. Bolometric interferometry therefore aims at mitigating the scalability limitations of coherent interferometry while preserving its strong control of systematic effects. In addition, bolometers provide significantly higher sensitivity than coherent amplifiers at CMB frequencies ($[100 - 300]~\mathrm{GHz}$). 
Nevertheless, several important challenges remain. Bolometric interferometers rely on complex optical systems involving many elements (large horn arrays, \steve{\sout{mirrors}} \jc{optical combiner}, switches, etc.), increasing both instrumental complexity and potential systematic effects. Moreover, the synthesized beam produced by the instrument is significantly more complicated than in direct imagers and requires extremely precise characterisation for high-precision cosmological measurements. \steve{However, the complexity associated with the horn array will improve the capabilities of QUBIC to achieve its scientific goal}. \steve{\sout{Since this architecture has never been deployed at the scale of modern imaging experiments, uncertainties on its scalability still remain.}}

\jc{\sout{The QUBIC experiment was designed to demonstrate the advantages of bolometric interferometry and evaluate its relevance for primordial B-mode polarisation observations.}}
\jc{The QUBIC experiment was designed to explore the possibility of constraining B-modes with Bolometric Interferometry, exploiting its $2$ unique features : self-calibration and spectral imaging.}

\section{QUBIC Experiment}

\subsection{QUBIC Collaboration}

During the 2000s, multiple projects started to develop around the concept of Bolometric Interferometry. We can cite Millimetre-wave Bolometric Interferometer (MBI)~\cite{MBI2002, MBI2003}, Einstein Polarization Interferometer for Cosmology (EPIC)~\cite{EPIC}, Background RAdiation INterferometer (BRAIN)~\cite{BRAIN} or CMBPol~\cite{CMBPol}. The different members of these collaborations joined together to work on a common project : the Q \& U Bolometric Interferometer for Cosmology\footnote{\url{https://www.qubic.org.ar/}} (QUBIC)~\cite{preQUBICI,ghribiPhD,preQUBICII,preQUBICIII, QUBICI}. QUBIC is an international collaboration, with participations from France, Italy, Argentina, Ireland, the USA, the UK, and more recently Morocco.

\subsection{Observing Site} 

An observing site dedicated to CMB polarisation measurements must satisfy two main constraints. First, like any astronomical observatory, it must be located far from major sources of light pollution. Second, it must be situated at high altitude and in an extremely dry region. These latter requirements are intended to minimise atmospheric water vapour, which constitutes one of the main source of noises\footnote{\jc{The total optical power received by a detector can be written as the sum of the contributions from the sky and the atmosphere, $P_{\mathrm{det}} = P_{\mathrm{sky}} + P_{\mathrm{atm}}$. Since the atmospheric loading dominates the sky signal, $P_{\mathrm{atm}} \gg P_{\mathrm{sky}}$, the associated photon noise is also dominated by the atmosphere, leading approximately to $\sigma_{\mathrm{det}} \propto \sqrt{P_{\mathrm{det}}} \simeq \sqrt{P_{\mathrm{atm}}}$.}} for ground-based observations (see \ref{subsection:atm} and \ref{chap:atm}), since the water vapour content decreases rapidly with altitude. Only a few privileged observing sites exist worldwide, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:best_obs_sites}: the high-altitude regions of the Andes between Chile and Argentina, the Tibetan Plateau, Greenland, and the South Pole.
From an atmospheric perspective, the South Pole, where experiments such as BICEP2 and Keck Array are located, provides exceptionally favourable observing conditions thanks to its extremely low PWV and highly stable atmosphere, despite its relatively modest altitude. However, the logistical challenges associated with operating in Antarctica (transportation, power supply, extremely low temperatures, limited repair capabilities, and restricted human presence) reduce the attractiveness of the site. Political and environmental considerations, governed by the Antarctic Treaty System, complicate the deployment of future facilities.\footnote{For example, discussions surrounding the implementation of next-generation projects such as CMB-S4 have highlighted the increasing logistical and environmental constraints associated with Antarctic deployment.} In addition, only a limited fraction of the sky is observable from the South Pole, making large sky surveys impossible from this location. On the other hand, the same sky patch remains continuously visible, enabling uninterrupted long-duration observations, unlike at lower latitudes where targets rise and set during the day.

The most privileged observing site is the Atacama Desert in the Chilean Andes, where experiments such as Atacama Cosmology Telescope, Simons Observatory, POLARBEAR, Simons Array, CLASS, and QUIET (as well as several non-CMB facilities) are located. This region is among the driest on Earth outside Antarctica, providing excellent atmospheric conditions for millimetre and sub-millimetre observations. Chile also has a long-standing history of astronomical collaboration, which has enabled the development of robust infrastructure and stable logistical support in this region~\cite{Atacama}. On the Argentine side of the Andes, the Alto Chorrillos region\footnote{The Atacama Desert and Alto Chorrillos are separated by approximately $\sim 300,\mathrm{km}$.} hosts experiments such as QUBIC, as shown Figure \ref{fig:qubic_obs_site} and the LLAMA project. This site shares very similar atmospheric characteristics with the Atacama plateau, including high altitude and low precipitable water vapour. However, differences in long-term investment in astronomical infrastructure and the maturity of large-scale observatory ecosystems result in a less developed operational environment compared to northern Chile.
In the Northern Hemisphere, Greenland and Tibet are also promising high-altitude or high-latitude sites for millimetre astronomy~\cite{tibet_greenland}. Greenland suffers from similar logistical limitations to the South Pole, particularly in terms of infrastructure and accessibility, despite excellent atmospheric conditions. Tibet, while offering high-altitude dry sites, faces challenges related to infrastructure development and long-term operational and political stability, which currently limit its use for large-scale CMB observatories.

\subsection{Observation Sky Patch} \label{sub:sky_patch}

For scientific survey, the observed region on the sky matters. Experiments observing a large fraction of the sky are generally interested in large scale effects or high statistics on the data. On the opposite, some collaborations are observing only a small fraction of the sky, which is done to achieve higher signal over noise ratio. QUBIC is scheduled to be in the second case, observing a $\sim 1.5^\circ$ patch located at $(RA=0^\circ, DEC=57^\circ)$ during $3$ years, as explained in \cite{QUBICI}. The main advantage of this sky patch is the low contamination from dust and synchrotron~\cite{Krachmalnicoff_2016}, as well as being visible from QUBIC observing site $\sim 40\%$ of the time. \subsection{Science Objectives}

\subsubsection{CMB Polarisation}

As suggested by its name, the QUBIC instrument was specifically designed to measure CMB polarisation. Several instrumental features were developed for this purpose. They are briefly introduced here and discussed in detail throughout this thesis.

The first requirement for detecting primordial CMB B-modes is a very high sensitivity. QUBIC achieves this using Transition Edge Sensors (TES)~\cite{QUBICIV}, a specific class of bolometric detectors described in Subsection \ref{sub:TES}. At the same time, an excellent control of instrumental systematics is mandatory. QUBIC plans to use \emph{self-calibration}, a technique widely employed in radio interferometry, which consists in comparing signals measured by different baselines in order to constrain instrumental systematics~\cite{paper_selfcal_1,paper_selfcal_2,selfcal_overview,paper_marie_selfcal}. This method is discussed in Chapter \ref{chap:gps}, and more specifically in Subsection \ref{sub:selfcal}.

Another key feature of QUBIC is the use of a polarising grid coupled to full power detectors. This architecture strongly suppresses instrumental cross-polarisation effects~\cite{QUBICVI}; further details are provided in Subsection \ref{sub:polariser}.

\subsubsection{Foreground Mitigation}

Separating the CMB polarisation signal from astrophysical foreground contamination relies primarily on the different spectral dependences of the various emissions. In practice, this requires observations over multiple frequency channels in order to disentangle the different contributions.

For traditional imagers, this leads to a significant increase in instrumental complexity and cost, since dedicated detector arrays are generally required for each frequency band. Bolometric interferometers, however, naturally exhibit a frequency-dependent synthesized beam, allowing the spectral information to be spatially encoded across the focal plane. This unique feature, referred to as \emph{spectral imaging}, is one of the main advantages of bolometric interferometry. It is presented in detail in Chapter \ref{chap:spectral_imaging}, and constitutes the core of this thesis, as all chapters in Part \ref{part:MM} rely on this concept.

It is also important to note that an instrument designed to separate CMB emission from astrophysical foregrounds must necessarily be highly sensitive to the properties of these foreground emissions themselves. In this respect, QUBIC could also provide valuable measurements for Galactic science, even though this is not part of its primary scientific program.

\subsubsection{Demonstrating Bolometric Interferometry}

QUBIC is a unique instrument employing a technology that is entirely novel within the field of CMB observations. As such, it serves as a proof of concept for bolometric interferometry, demonstrating both its capabilities and its potential for future generations of CMB experiments.

In this Section, the full instrumental design of QUBIC is described, as the knowledge of the instrumental design is mandatory to understand the behaviours of QUBIC. 
Before entering into the details of the different electronic and optic element, here is a quick resume of the instrumental design of QUBIC. Photons enter the instrument through a 56 cm transparent window, before being filtered to eliminate frequencies outside the observation bandwidths, to avoid being heated by high-energy photons. Then, photons enter inside QUBIC's cryostat~\cite{QUBICV}. The light polarisation is modulated by a rotating Half-Wave Plate (HWP), before passing through a polariser to select a single polarisation direction, the resulting signal being transmitted to the array of 400 back-to-back horns~\cite{QUBICVII}, at the 4K stage. The 400 split signals will then be reflected by 2 concave mirrors at 1K, to be focused on the focal plane where the 992 TESs~\cite{QUBICIV} cooled down at $\sim 320$ mK are located, to produce an interference pattern. QUBIC Instrument will observe the two frequency atmospheric windows at 150 \& 220 GHz. 


\subsection{Technical Demonstrator}

In order to validate the instrumental concept of QUBIC, including bolometric interferometry, the cryogenic system, optics, detectors, and readout electronics, a Technical Demonstrator (TD) was constructed. The TD uses the same cryostat as the Full Instrument (FI), but with a reduced optical configuration: 64 horns instead of 400, a smaller focal plane (one quarter of the full size), and smaller mirrors. The TD operates in a single frequency band at $150~GHz$. Its primary objective is to validate the full end-to-end functionality of all QUBIC subsystems and to demonstrate the feasibility of bolometric interferometry under realistic observational conditions on the sky.

\steve{To transition from the TD to the FI, several components still need to be manufactured, tested, and installed. While the horn array, bolometers, and readout electronics have already been produced, the bolometers are still undergoing testing, and additional cabling for the readout electronics remains to be integrated. The mechanical switches for the horn configuration have not yet been manufactured, and additional optical components such as filters and the half-wave plate (HWP) have been ordered and are expected to be delivered within the year. This staging enables a relatively straightforward transition from the demonstrator phase to the full-scale instrument once on-site integration is completed.}

\subsection{Window \& Filters}

QUBIC observes the sky through a 56 cm of diameter window, made with multi-layer high density polyethylene. This material is transparent to millimetre wavelengths, while being opaque to infrared signals to avoid thermal load in the instrument~\cite{Choi_2013}. The aperture is followed by a series of filters which aims at cutting frequencies outside the observing windows allowed by atmosphere, between 130 and 245 GHz, with a notch filter to cut between 170 and 200 GHz (see Subsection \ref{subsection:atm}). The TD filters only allow for observations between 130 and 170 GHz.

\subsection{Half-Wave Plate \& polariser} \label{sub:polariser}

Right after the filters, two optical elements dealing with polarisation are placed: the Half-Wave Plate (HWP)~\cite{QUBICVI} and the polariser. They are both cooled down to $4\,\mathrm{K}$ to limit the thermal load of the instrument.

The HWP rotates in a step-by-step manner and modulates the sky signal depending on its rotation angle. After the HWP, the electric field
\[
\vec{E} =
\begin{pmatrix}
E_x \\
E_y
\end{pmatrix}
\]
becomes:

\begin{equation}
    \vec{E}' =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        E_x \cos(2\varphi(t)) + E_y \sin(2\varphi(t)) \\
        E_x \sin(2\varphi(t)) - E_y \cos(2\varphi(t))
    \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $\varphi$ is the rotation angle of the HWP.

The motor is placed outside the cryostat, and its rotation is transmitted to the HWP via a glass fibre axis magnetically connected to a pulley system, rotating the plate with a belt (see Figure~\ref{fig:qubic_hwp}).

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{hpw_model.jpeg}
    \caption{3D model of the Half-Wave Plate rotator mechanism. Taken from \cite{QUBICVI}.}
    \label{fig:qubic_hwp}
\end{figure}

After the HWP, the signal enters a polariser, which transmits only the component aligned with its axis, effectively selecting a single linear polarisation state. The field becomes:
\begin{equation}
    \vec{E}' =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        E_x \cos(2\varphi(t)) + E_y \sin(2\varphi(t)) \\
        0
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

The resulting detected signal mixes intensity and polarisation, allowing reconstruction with intensity-only detectors from trigonometric formula:
\begin{align}
    S &= |\vec{E}|^2 \notag \\
      &= E_x^2 \cos^2(Z\varphi) + E_y^2 \sin^2(2\varphi) + 2E_x E_y \cos(2\varphi)\sin(2\varphi) \notag \\
      &= E_x^2 \frac{\left(1+\cos(4\varphi)\right)}{2} + E_y^2 \frac{\left(1-\cos(4\varphi)\right)}{2} + E_x E_y \sin(4\varphi) \notag \\
      &= \frac{1}{2} \left(E_x^2 + E_y^2\right) + \frac{1}{2}\cos(4\varphi) \left(E_x^2 - E_y^2\right) E_x E_y \sin(4\varphi) \notag \\
    S &= \frac{1}{2} \left(I + Q\cos(4\varphi) + U\sin(4\varphi)\right).
\end{align}

The fixed $7$ positions for the HWM, separated by $15^{~\circ}$~\cite{QUBICVI}, and associated signals are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:hwp_signal}.

Although it may seem wasteful to discard half of the incoming photons with the polariser, this is a key instrumental choice. By selecting a single polarisation state early in the optical chain and using intensity-only detectors, the instrument becomes significantly less sensitive to internal cross-polarisation effects arising from optical components such as horns and mirrors, as well as from reflections within the instrument downstream of the polariser. Since mitigating such systematics would otherwise require substantial effort, it is preferable to sacrifice part of the incoming signal in exchange for improved control of the instrument's polarisation response.
The effectiveness of this design has been demonstrated in \cite{QUBICIII}, where the instrumental cross-polarisation was measured to be below 0.4\% at the 95\% confidence level. This value is significantly lower than the percent-level cross-polarisation typically reported for many classical CMB imagers, highlighting one of the main advantages of the QUBIC optical architecture.

\subsection{Horns Array}

The next and key part of the interferometric design of QUBIC is the horns array~\cite{QUBICVII}. It consists in 400 back-to-back corrugated horns at $4$ K, separated by $14$ mm. Corrugations are periodic grooves machined inside the horn walls, meant to improve beam symmetry and reduce cross-polarisation systematics, are shown in Figure \ref{fig:horn}. Both back-to-back horns are designed to be identical, to observe the sky and illuminate the optical combiner with a Full-Width at Half-Maximum (FWHM) of 12.9° at $150$ GHz. Between them is a mechanical switch, thought to remotely close or open each horn in the array separately, a mandatory feature to perform self-calibration~\cite{paper_marie_selfcal} (see \ref{sub:selfcal}).

\subsection{Optical Combiner}  

The multiple beams produced by the horn arrays then illuminate the optical combiner~\cite{QUBICVIII}, two concave mirrors, to focus light into the focal plan, producing an interference pattern. The two mirrors have a diameter of $600$ mm and the system has a focal length of $30$ cm. They are cooled down at $1$ K.

\subsection{Bolometers} \label{sub:TES}

The focal plane of QUBIC is made of an array of bolometers, which measure light intensity by measuring the modification of resistivity due to the modification of temperature induced by the absorption of photons. In particular, QUBIC is using Transition Edge Sensor (TES)~\cite{QUBICIV}, chosen for their high sensitivity. These detectors, made of NbSi, are placed at the transition of superconductivity, around $300$ mK. At this temperature, the resistance shows high resistance variation for small temperature modification, as visible in Figure \ref{fig:TES}. This figure shows measurements of resistivity for four TESs at different temperatures. Resistance is close to $0 \Omega$ below $0.47$ K, showing a superconductivity state. The transition occurs between $0.47$ K and $0.49$ K, we can see a quick variation of resistance with temperature in this region, with compare to the region above $0.5$ K. \footnote{This measurement comes from the TES sample P41, while the one currently used for the TD is the sample P87, with different properties (in particular, a different critical temperature). This plot is used only for understanding purposes.}
