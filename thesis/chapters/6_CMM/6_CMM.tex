\chapter{Component Map-Making} \label{chap:CMM}

This chapter introduces an innovative algorithm that performs astrophysical component separation simultaneously with map-making by exploiting the frequency information contained in QUBIC Time-Ordered-Data. Although applicable to any CMB experiment, this method is particularly effective when combined with the spectral imaging capabilities enabled by bolometric interferometry, which allows for an increased spectral resolution. We refer to this approach as \emph{Component Map-Making} (CMM).

The first section presents the general principles of CMM and highlights its differences with Frequency Map-Making (FMM). We then discuss the component-separation formalism, for which two approaches have been implemented: one based on parametric models of foreground emission and another independent of any specific foreground model. The chapter concludes with a discussion of the numerical properties of CMM and the statistical properties of the reconstructed maps. This chapter focuses on the methodology; detailed developments and results are presented in the following chapters.

\section{General Principle}

The previous chapter introduced the Frequency Map-Making algorithm, which exploits QUBIC's spectral imaging capabilities to reconstruct multiple frequency maps of the sky. The primary motivation for the CMM approach is to improve component separation by increasing both the number of available frequency channels and the spectral resolution. Since the spectral information is already encoded in the time-ordered data, it is natural to ask whether component separation can be performed directly during map reconstruction, without first reconstructing frequency maps.

Based on this idea, we developed a new map-making framework that simultaneously reconstructs the emission maps of the different astrophysical components while separating their contributions using the spectral information contained in the TOD. In contrast to FMM, where the unknowns are sky maps at different frequencies, the unknowns of CMM are directly the component maps themselves.

This conceptual modification of the map-making formalism leads to the following data model:

\begin{equation}
\vec{d} = H A \vec{c} + \vec{n},
\end{equation}

where $A$ is the mixing matrix and $\vec{c}$, the component maps, with dimensions $(N_{\mathrm{comp}}, N_{\mathrm{pix}}, N_{\mathrm{stokes}})$. This equation is equivalent to equation \ref{eq:acquisition}, with $\vec{s} \equiv A\vec{c}$.

The mixing matrix $A$, of dimensions $(N_{\mathrm{freq}}, N_{\mathrm{comp}})$, provides the link between astrophysical components and frequency channels. This formalism relies on the assumption that each astrophysical component can be described by a spatial template whose amplitude varies with frequency according to a spectral law. Although this assumption is restrictive, it is necessary to make the problem realisable. In this model, $N_{freq}$ is the number of frequency bins within the physical bandwidth of QUBIC. The same way as with $N_{rec}$ in FMM, increasing $N_{freq}$ will improve the component separation capabilities (in particular in case of complex foregrounds), but will also increase the reconstruction noise because of the anti-correlation between sub-bands. This parameter needs to be chosen wisely, depending on the model considered for foregrounds. With component maps expressed in $K_{CMB}$, the CMB column in the mixing matrix is filled with $1$.

Since both $A$ and $\vec{c}$ are unknown and strongly correlated, they cannot be estimated simultaneously using a standard PCG solver. In principle, a non-linear PCG could be implemented to jointly estimate both quantities. However, such an approach would require substantial algorithmic developments and is beyond the scope of the present work. Instead, we adopt an alternating optimisation scheme. The principle is to fix one quantity while estimating the other, and then alternate between the two estimation steps. Repeating this procedure iteratively allows the algorithm to progressively converge towards a joint solution for the component maps and the mixing matrix.

The component maps are still reconstructed using PCG method, in the exact same way than in FMM algorithm. On another hand, mixing matrix is estimated with a classical $\chi^2$ minimisation, done in two different manners, details in the following Sections.

\noindent
\textbf{Remark:} In \cite{CMM}, we also explored the possibility of jointly reconstructing instrumental systematic effects together with the mixing matrix and the component maps. As a proof of concept, detector gains were included among the fitted parameters. Instrumental systematic estimation within the CMM is not considered further in this thesis, since self-calibration (discussed in Chapter~\ref{chap}) provides a dedicated framework for handling instrumental uncertainties. The detectors' gain estimation was shown as a proof of concept.

\section{Parametric Reconstruction}

\subsection{Principle}

The parametric reconstruction of the mixing matrix relies on the foregrounds' model to separate their contribution from CMB. Therefore, each mixing matrix element is described by a function $f_{comp} \left(\nu, \theta_{comp}\right)$, where $\theta_{comp}$ are the model parameters to be estimated. The estimation of $\theta_{comp}$ for each component reconstructed is enough to characterise the entire mixing matrix $A$. These parameters are fitted using a $chi^2$ minimisation :

\begin{equation}
    \chi^2(\theta_{comp}) = \left(\vec{d}-HA\left(\theta_{comp}\right)\vec{c}\right)^{~T} N^{-1} \left(\vec{d}-HA\left(\theta_{comp}\right)\vec{c}\right),
\end{equation}

where $\vec{d}$ is the observed TOD, $H$ the instrument simulation model and $\vec{c}$ the component maps estimated through PCG (but fixed here). The function is minimised using simple gradient descent with the \emph{minimize} function of SciPy library\footnote{\url{https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.optimize.minimize.html}}.

This corresponds to the \emph{Parametric CMM}.

\section{Blind Reconstruction}

\subsection{Principle}

While developing the Parametric CMM, we saw the limitation of relying on a given model to mitigate foregrounds contamination of CMB signal. Indeed, if we wrongly assume the model to describe foregrounds, the component separation will be biased, resulting in wrong cosmological parameters fitting with CMB map. Deeper than that, in case of complex foregrounds model, with random underlying effects, we will never be able to correctly estimate them. This thought is the basis of the development of non-parametric component separation method.
Based on this idea, we developed a non-parametric version of the CMM, that we call \emph{Blind CMM}.

Here, we are not considering any parameter for foregrounds, the mixing matrix elements are now the unknowns we aim at fitting. It drastically increases the complexity of the reconstruction : while $N_{comp}-1$ parameters were enough to describe the full mixing matrix in the Parametric case, $\left(N_{comp}-1\right)\times N_{freq}$ are now required in the Blind approach. The increased complexity will affect the reconstruction, but is the only way to separate complex foregrounds model from CMB. Beyond that, thanks to QUBIC's high spectral resolution, unbiased reconstruction is possible, completely independently of the real foregrounds' model. The case of frequency decorrelated dust is explore in \cite{regnier2024}, and discuss in subsection \ref{sub:frequency_decorr}.

\section{Characterisation}

\subsection{Numerical complexity}

CMM inherits the same fundamental computational limitations as FMM in terms of memory usage and execution time, since both methods rely on the same instrumental model $H$. Consequently, the numerical constraints associated with the storage and manipulation of the acquisition operator are identical to those discussed in subsection \ref{sub:FMM_numerical_limits}. However, the implementation of CMM is significantly more involved than that of FMM because it combines component separation and map-making within a single framework. In addition to the map reconstruction process, the algorithm must handle the estimation of foreground spectral parameters, multiple reconstruction strategies, and various foreground models. As an illustration of this increased implementation complexity, the core CMM software contains approximately 4000 lines of code, compared to about 1600 for the FMM implementation, excluding the simulation of the QUBIC instrument and the acquisition operator $H$.

Beyond software complexity, the main computational challenge of CMM arises from the alternating optimisation procedure used to jointly estimate the component maps and the mixing matrix. This approach is necessary because both quantities are unknown and strongly correlated, preventing the direct application of a linear solver. At each iteration, the estimation of one quantity is performed while keeping the other fixed. Since the fixed quantity generally differs from its true value, the resulting estimate is biased, and the algorithm progresses through a sequence of incremental corrections.
As a consequence, convergence is typically slower than in FMM, where the PCG algorithm directly minimises a linear objective function. Furthermore, the parameter space explored by CMM is non-linear and may exhibit degeneracies between component maps and spectral parameters. This increases the risk of convergence towards local minima and generally requires a larger number of iterations to reach a stable solution.
The convergence properties of CMM are also strongly dependent on the initialisation of the reconstruction, particularly on the initial values chosen for the mixing matrix parameters. Poor initialisation may significantly increase the convergence time or lead to suboptimal solutions.
These limitations motivate the development of non-linear optimisation methods capable of jointly estimating the component maps and spectral parameters. Such approaches could substantially improve both the robustness and efficiency of the reconstruction and represent a promising direction for future developments of the CMM framework.

Similar to the FMM, the angular resolution of the reconstructed maps is a complicated part. We again explore two approaches : one in which we impose the reconstructed resolution and another one where we let it free.

\subsubsection{Fixed angular resolution reconstruction}

In FMM (see subsection \ref{sub:fmm_convolution}), we fixed the reconstucted resolution at the best resolution per reconstructed sub-bands, to ensure the best possible results. In CMM, we no longer reconstruct frequency maps, but component maps at a reference frequency. Following the same reasoning, we reconstruct the component maps at the best resolution for QUBIC inside its physical band, meaning the resolution at the highest frequency. Following that, during reconstruction, each sub-bands will be convolved at the resolution $\sigma_{\nu_j, best} = \sqrt{\sigma_{\nu_j} - \sigma_{best}}$, with $\sigma_{\nu_j}$ QUBIC's FWHM at frequency $\nu_j$ and $\sigma_{best}$ the smallest FWHM within QUBIC's band. The data acquisition equation is then written :

\begin{equation}
    \vec{\tilde{d}} = \int_{\nu_{min}}^{\nu_{max}} \mathcal{H}_\nu C_\nu A_{\nu} \vec{\tilde{c}}^{~best} d\nu + \vec{n},
\end{equation}

with $C_\nu$ the convolution operator at resolution $\sigma_{best}$ and $\vec{\tilde{c}}^{~best}$ the component maps at best resolution.
